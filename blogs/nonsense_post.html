Choosing the nonsense I work on

November 4, 2026

A friend is currently interning at Google Research. She asked a more senior team member for advice about what career paths to follow – whether to continue working in “big tech”, pursue a PhD, or join a startup. At the last option, the team member smiled and said, “they are all working on varying degrees of the same nonsense.” This interaction sparked a deeper question for me: How do I choose my degree of nonsense?


As someone in the trenches of my postdoctoral fellowship period, this is an increasingly important question: How do I choose which questions to dedicate my time to, how do I discern which career options to pursue, and how do I best prepare myself for that looming career transition? But this is an equally urgent question for anyone working with state-of-the-art artificial intelligence models. These models have given us unprecedented implementation and scaling power, making it easy to get lost in the weeds and continue aimlessly exploring the vast hypothesis and question space. Below, I lay out a two-part framework for choosing our nonsense, one that applies to research questions and career decisions alike.


Pillar 1: Chase the idea, not the hype. In 2020, new models with greater benchmark performance were dropping nearly daily. I was an aspiring cognitive computational neuroscientist using human data to try and make sense of how learning happens in the brain. I was even more excited about models that seemed to perform on par with human benchmarks. For me, it was all too easy to get caught up in the hype of these models – their creation and benchmark accuracy undoubtedly represented an incredible engineering feat, but what did it really mean for human learning? I was confronted by this as I presented a paper in journal club with the main takeaway that model X significantly outperformed previous models on certain benchmarks. My PI immediately stopped me to ask, “and what does this tell you about the brain?” I sat there on Zoom thinking quietly for a few seconds and then replied “nothing”. That was the shortest lab presentation I have ever given. I was chasing the hype and not the idea: As a neuroscientist, building models for the sake of building models is exciting, and it is even more exciting when they approach or surpass human benchmark performance. But unless it advanced my end goal, in my case a deeper understanding of human cognitive mechanisms in learning, then so what?


Today, there are even more flashy distractors, no matter what field you belong in. For folks using AI agents like Claude Code to run research analyses, there are often infinite methods that can be applied to the same data. It is increasingly important to make sure that we pursue ideas that advance our end goal, instead of just following the latest research trends.


Pillar 2: Identify your bottom line. Hype is an external pull. There is an equally strong internal pull: the next interesting move. When starting a new research project, we typically have a general hypothesis to test, a problem we want to solve, or an overall “goal”. However, getting into the weeds of the data can reveal additional avenues to pursue. At some point, it becomes necessary to take a step back and sketch out the larger picture: What story are the data telling us and what does each individual avenue contribute to it? Often, we find that the data tell a more interesting or nuanced story than we had initially imagined. We then need to triage our results: (1) Which of these avenues contribute to our original question? (2) Which reveal a critical, unexpected finding to pursue? (3) Which, while interesting, are peripheral to the larger story? Sorting results into groups #1 and #3 may be relatively straightforward, but how do we decide whether to pursue results that fall into group #2?


In research, we have a relatively structured framework for making these decisions. We can evaluate unexpected findings against broader scientific objectives, consider their significance and then determine whether pursuing them advances our underlying intellectual motivations. The emergence of AI agents capable of conducting increasingly complex analyses makes this exercise even more important. As the number of investigative avenues we can explore grows, so does the need to decide which are worth pursuing.


This act of filtering can be applied to other aspects of our lives: While we may have multiple directions available to us, we need a system for being able to decide which direction to pursue, especially when they seem equally worthwhile and their payoff is not immediately clear. For life decisions (e.g., career moves) extending beyond research, identifying the bottom line can be a much harder task: We are not always provided with clear objectives against which to evaluate our options. I think we need to first identify a core series of “values” that we hold and then pursue activities that let us exercise these values. What matters may not be the specific career we choose but rather whether that career lets us pursue what we find meaningful.


We must choose our own nonsense to pursue every day. If it all really is varying degrees of the same nonsense, then what matters is what we get out of it. We can choose more meaningfully by identifying our own bottom line and then chasing projects, ideas, and opportunities that allow us to put our values into practice.
