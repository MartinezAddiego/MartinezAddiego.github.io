What Happened to Sora?

October 22, 2026

“Fail quickly.” In research, we use this phrase to describe our approach when trying (at most times) far-fetched ideas: test them early, learn from results, and move on quickly when you reach a dead end. It is a useful principle for approaching exploratory research, offering a high-risk, high-reward scenario where even failures can produce valuable knowledge. But, this same logic is generally not something that can be readily applied to consumer products. Users and investors generally do not want experimentation: they want products that yield consistent results.


OpenAI’s Sora 2 offers an interesting case study for failing quickly. Sora 2 was initially released on September 30, 2025 [1], and its discontinuation was announced a little under 6 months later, in March 2026 [2]. Examining why Sora 2 failed a critical stress test is a useful data point for interpreting the progress of “big tech” artificial intelligence companies.


When Sora 2 was first introduced, descriptions were overwhelmingly positive and ambitious. Sam Altman wrote in his blog, that “[Sora is a] ChatGPT for creativity moment” that “[can drastically increase] the quality of art and entertainment” [3]. Sora’s use-case went beyond high-quality generation of realistic and physically plausible videos. OpenAI framed it as a new social media application that mimicked Instagram and Facebook Reels or TikTok [1]. Yet, just 103 days after announcing a proposed $1 billion partnership with Disney [4], Sora shut down. What happened? Reporting from the Wall Street Journal suggests that Sora was not profitable [5], between the steep price tag (up to $200/month), the sharp decrease in users [6], and the actual computational daily cost for user video generation [7], the numbers just did not add up. I think, however, there is a more important lesson behind this. Rather than using AI for facilitating creative expression, OpenAI tried to convert AI-generated content into a personalized social media experience. In this case, attracting and retaining users was necessary for the product’s success. In the rush to be first, in the rush to innovate, and in the rush to compete and stand out, OpenAI created a product that, in my opinion, was reduced to TikTok-like AI slop.


The financial failure of Sora 2 is only one part of the story. Sora 2’s brief existence raises critical questions about the consequences of releasing powerful AI technologies before we fully understand how their use may diverge from their intended purpose. Indeed, Sora 2 faced a series of ethical issues ranging from DeepFakes and copyright infringement to nonconsensual use of individuals’ likeness. While certain safety measures were published, the truth is that these measures were simply insufficient to prevent serious misuse. OpenAI may have failed quickly with Sora 2, but unlike in research, it remains unclear whether lessons learned from this failure will meaningfully shape the way we develop, deploy, and use future AI technology. AI development continues to race forward at alarming speeds towards a critical juncture. At some point, we need to consciously decide what should dictate the pace of innovation, whether it should be profit and recognition or instead the study and placement of ethical safeguards. I hope, going forward, we choose to fail quickly the way we do in research: with results that are documented and shared, that meaningfully shape the next steps, and with directions that are quickly abandoned when shown to not be fruitful.
